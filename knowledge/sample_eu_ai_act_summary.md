# EU AI Act Summary (Sample)

> Placeholder paraphrase of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 for testing Ben. Not the official text.
> Always check the Official Journal version and current implementation guidance.

## Scope and timeline

The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Prohibited practices apply from 2 February 2025, obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models apply from 2 August 2025, and most remaining obligations, including those for Annex III high-risk systems, apply from 2 August 2026. Implementation dates may be adjusted by later EU legislation, so confirm the current position with Legal.

The Act applies to providers placing AI systems on the EU market and to deployers using AI systems in the EU, including non-EU organisations whose system output is used in the EU.

## Article 5 — Prohibited AI practices

Certain practices are banned outright, including: manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm; exploiting vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation; social scoring leading to unjustified detrimental treatment; emotion recognition in the workplace and in education (except for medical or safety reasons); untargeted scraping of facial images to build facial recognition databases; and certain uses of real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement.

## Article 6 — Classification of high-risk AI systems

An AI system is high-risk if it is a safety component of a product covered by EU harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I and requires third-party conformity assessment, or if it falls within one of the use cases in Annex III. An Annex III system is not high-risk if it does not pose a significant risk of harm, for example because it performs only a narrow procedural task or a preparatory task; this exception never applies where the system performs profiling of natural persons. Providers relying on the exception must document their assessment.

## Annex III — High-risk use cases

Annex III lists areas including: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education and vocational training; employment, workers management and access to self-employment; access to essential private and public services (for example creditworthiness assessment and life and health insurance pricing); law enforcement; migration, asylum and border control; and administration of justice and democratic processes.

The employment area (point 4) covers AI systems intended to be used for recruitment or selection, in particular to place targeted job adverts, to analyse and filter job applications (for example CV screening), and to evaluate candidates, as well as systems used to make decisions on promotion, termination, task allocation or to monitor and evaluate performance and behaviour.

## Article 9 — Risk management system

Providers of high-risk AI systems must establish, document and maintain a risk management system that runs throughout the lifecycle. It must identify and analyse known and reasonably foreseeable risks to health, safety and fundamental rights, estimate and evaluate risks including under reasonably foreseeable misuse, and adopt targeted risk management measures. Residual risk must be judged acceptable, and testing must be performed against predefined metrics.

## Article 10 — Data and data governance

Training, validation and testing data sets for high-risk systems must be subject to data governance practices covering design choices, data collection and origin, preparation, assumptions, availability and suitability, examination for possible biases, and identification of data gaps. Data sets must be relevant, sufficiently representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete in view of the intended purpose.

## Article 13 — Transparency and provision of information to deployers

High-risk systems must be designed so that deployers can interpret the output and use it appropriately. They must come with instructions for use covering the provider's identity, the system's characteristics, capabilities and limitations, accuracy and robustness levels, human oversight measures and expected lifetime.

## Article 14 — Human oversight

High-risk systems must be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee them during use. Oversight measures should let the people assigned understand the system's capacities and limitations, remain aware of automation bias, correctly interpret output, decide not to use or to override the output, and interrupt the system through a stop procedure.

## Article 26 — Obligations of deployers of high-risk AI systems

Deployers must use high-risk systems in accordance with the instructions for use, assign human oversight to competent and trained people, ensure input data under their control is relevant and sufficiently representative, monitor operation and report serious incidents, keep automatically generated logs for at least six months, and inform workers' representatives and affected workers before putting a high-risk system into service in the workplace.

## Article 27 — Fundamental rights impact assessment

Certain deployers (bodies governed by public law, private entities providing public services, and deployers of some Annex III point 5 systems such as credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing) must perform a fundamental rights impact assessment before first use. It describes the processes in which the system is used, the period and frequency of use, the categories of persons affected, specific risks of harm, human oversight measures and the measures to take if risks materialise.

## Article 50 — Transparency obligations for certain AI systems

Providers must ensure AI systems that interact directly with people (such as chatbots) are designed so that people are informed they are interacting with an AI system, unless obvious from the context. Providers of generative AI systems must mark synthetic audio, image, video or text output in a machine-readable format so that it is detectable as artificially generated. Deployers of deep fakes must disclose that content has been artificially generated or manipulated.

## Article 53 — Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models

Providers of GPAI models must draw up and keep up to date technical documentation, provide information and documentation to downstream providers who integrate the model, put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training. GPAI models with systemic risk carry additional obligations under Article 55, including model evaluation, adversarial testing, serious incident reporting and cybersecurity protection.
